OFAC / US DOJ · 27 Oct · Potentially relevant

Extreme Networks: screening the name on the paperwork was not enough

What changed: OFAC announced a $1.53 million settlement over nine apparent violations involving networking equipment and services for a Russian defence manufacturer blocked through majority ownership. The US DOJ separately resolved its investigation through a non-prosecution agreement. [S1] [S2]

Relevance to a Swiss IAM. High as a control lesson where an IAM relies on third parties for client, ownership or payment information. The settlement binds the named company; it does not create a new Swiss duty.

How the arrangement escaped name screening

The paperwork named DEMZ, but OFAC found the real end user was the Russian defence manufacturer MMZA. Seven purchase orders gave a MMZA email address or a Moscow destination linked to it. The distributor twice supplied MMZA’s website and then substituted DEMZ’s site; the transactions were approved on an end-user declaration without resolving that conflict. MMZA staff also used their own company email addresses when registering under the other name. [S1, pp. 1–2]

Why a clean name check did not clear the transaction

MMZA was blocked through its majority ownership by designated Almaz Antey. Under the US rule, 50% or more aggregate direct or qualifying indirect ownership by blocked persons can block an entity even if it is not named on the list. This is a US test; apply the relevant Swiss or other jurisdiction’s ownership/control rule separately. [S1, pp. 1, 3–4] [S3]

The parallel cases are not the same count

OFAC described nine apparent violations and assessed a $1,529,685 settlement. DOJ’s statement describes 11 purchase orders and a non-prosecution agreement with $975,721.84 in fine and forfeiture. OFAC treated the matter as voluntarily disclosed; DOJ said disclosure was not timely under its own policy. These figures concern different proceedings and should not be treated as a combined cash amount. [S1] [S2]

Application to a Swiss IAM

If an introducer gives one legal name but payment instructions, company email domains or operating addresses point to another party, establish who actually benefits and how that party is owned before relying on the introducer’s declaration. A routine use of an intermediary, without such indicators or an applicable restriction, is not itself evidence of evasion. [S1, pp. 2, 4] [S3]

Action plan

Applicability
Sanctions Introducers
Relevant where a client, introducer or payment intermediary supplies conflicting information about the true beneficiary, end user or ownership chain.
Responsible person / function
Execution: designated AML Officer and outsourced compliance/AML provider for screening or payment triage. In-house Legal Counsel: US nexus, legal basis and provider terms. In-house Risk Manager: review of material findings; Directors: oversight where warranted.
Due / trigger
No new regulatory implementation deadline for a Swiss IAM. To be set internally; resolve material discrepancies before proceeding with a potentially affected instruction.
Status
Applicability to be confirmed

Application to the IAM

  1. Compare the named client or counterparty with payer, payee, account holder, beneficiary and corporate ownership where relevant. Investigate material discrepancies rather than clearing the named party alone. [S1, pp. 2, 4] [S3]
  2. Escalate unresolved inconsistencies to Legal and Compliance before an affected instruction. Confirm the actual legal nexus and applicable ownership/control test, and distinguish US law from a provider’s contractual requirements. [S1, pp. 3–5] [S3]
Evidence
The conflicting information, independent verification of the actual party and ownership chain, applicable sanctions basis and decision on the instruction.
