FATF · 30 October 2026 · AML

FATF removes four jurisdictions from its grey list

What changed: FATF removed Bulgaria, Côte d’Ivoire, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Monaco from increased monitoring. Eighteen jurisdictions remain; the composition of the high-risk list is unchanged. [S1] [S2] [S3]

Why this matters for a Swiss IAM. If one of the four jurisdictions affects a client, beneficial owner or regular payment corridor, or the IAM’s country-risk matrix uses FATF status, review that risk factor. Do not automatically downgrade the relationship or stop enhanced due diligence (EDD): other risk factors or requirements may still apply. [S2] [S4] [S5]

What changed at FATF

The four jurisdictions completed their action plans and FATF removed them after successful on-site visits. FATF says they will continue working with their regional bodies. The October grey list contains 18 jurisdictions, with no additions at this plenary. [S1, increased monitoring] [S2]

The high-risk list is separate. Its membership did not change: the DPRK and Iran remain subject to calls for countermeasures; Myanmar remains subject to a call for enhanced due diligence, not countermeasures. FATF updated its statements on Iran and Myanmar. [S1, call for action] [S3]

How to apply this to a client file

FATF does not call for EDD solely because a jurisdiction is on the grey list. It recommends taking the information into account in a risk-based assessment. Under Article 13(2) and (2bis) AMLO-FINMA, the financial intermediary determines which risk criteria matter for its business, sets them out in internal instructions and uses them to identify higher-risk relationships. Relevant geographic links can include a client’s or beneficial owner’s domicile, where business is conducted, and the origin or destination of frequent payments. FINMA Guidance 04/2026 addresses the methodology of money-laundering risk analysis for FinIA institutions as well as banks. [S2, introduction] [S4, Art. 13(2) and (2bis)] [S6]

Example: if a client resident in Monaco was flagged under an internal rule tied to FATF monitoring, revisit that input. If the relationship remains higher risk because of other factors, such as a complex ownership structure or payment flows that are inconsistent with the client profile, keep the relevant enhanced checks. Article 6(2)(c) AMLA requires clarifying the background and purpose of higher-risk transactions or relationships. A FATF delisting does not itself lift separate sanctions or change a bank’s independent risk policy. [S2] [S4] [S5, Art. 6]

Action plan

Applicability
AML
Review where the IAM or its AML provider uses FATF status in country scoring or the four jurisdictions affect existing relationships or payment patterns.
Responsible person / function
Execution: designated AML Officer, with the outsourced AML/compliance provider where it maintains the country inputs. Review: in-house Risk Manager; in-house Legal Counsel if the applicable rules or provider requirements are unclear. Directors: oversight or approval of material policy changes under the IAM’s governance.
Due / trigger
FATF statement published 30 October 2026. No new statutory deadline for the IAM follows from this announcement. Internal review date: To be set internally.
Status
Applicability to be confirmed
  1. Check where FATF grey-list status feeds into onboarding, periodic reviews or payment-risk criteria. Update that input where the methodology uses the current list, and align it with the outsourced AML provider. [S2] [S4, Art. 13(2bis)]
  2. Identify relationships for which one of the four countries affected the risk rating or EDD. Reassess the country factor alongside the other reasons for the classification; retain enhanced measures where the remaining risk or applicable rules call for them. [S2] [S4] [S5, Art. 6]
Evidence
For an applicable review: the revised country-risk input and rationale for retaining or changing any affected relationship’s classification or controls.
